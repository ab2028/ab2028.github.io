Avis et Carmina (The Bird and the Poems) Latin prose composition · 2026

Omnes aliae aves unam avem propter pennas pallidas deridebant, dicentes eam indignam amicitia inter eas. Avis sola quaerebat cur tam acerbe iudicaretur et quid facere posset ut amicitiam tandem mereret. Putavit, si verbis pulchris uteretur, vitia sua obtegere posset. Itaque unam ex suis pennis evulsit, in atramento tinxit, atque pro calamo usus est. Hac penna carmen scripsit.

All the other birds were mocking one bird on account of his dull feathers, saying that he was unworthy of friendship among them. Alone, the bird asked why he was judged so harshly and what he was able to do to finally get friendship. He thought that if he used beautiful words, he could hide his ugly flaws. And so, he plucked out one of his own feathers, dipped it in ink, and used it as a pen. With this feather he wrote a poem.

Cum omnes aves carmen laudarent, tamen non quaererent quis id scripisset. Ita avis aliud scripsit, atque aliud. Quotiens scribebat, unam pennam evellebat, donec corpus eius nudum esset.

Although all the birds praised the poem, still they did not ask who had written it. So the bird wrote another, and another. Each time he plucked a feather to write, until his body was bare.

Cum tandem corpus eius deplumis ante eas staret eumque poetam esse ostenderet, aves credere noluerunt. Dicebant poetam tam laudatum talem speciem habere non posse, atque verba a deis orta esse. Una ex eis clamavit, “Saltem antea pennas habebas; nunc ne volare quidem potes.” Carmina tenuerunt atque avem expulerunt.

When at last his featherless body stood before them, and revealed him to be the poet, the birds refused to believe him. They said that a poet so admired could not have such an appearance, and that the words had come from the gods. One of them cried, “At least before, you had feathers; now you cannot even fly.” They kept the poems and drove the bird away.

Commentarii de arte gladii (Commentaries on Fencing) Latin prose composition · 2026

In conclavi artis gladii, tres gentes sunt, quarum una appellatur Epee, altera Rudis, tertia Saber. Hae legibus, gladiis, animis inter se differunt, sed omnes eandem terram incolunt et ab eodem sacro iudice, qui arbiter appellatur, reguntur.

In the room of fencing, there are three tribes, one of which is called Epee, the second called Foil, and the third called Sabre. These tribes differ among themselves in laws, swords, and disposition, but all three tribes inhabit the same land and are ruled by the same sacred judge, who is called the referee.

Epera Martem sicut ceterae gentes venerantur. Cum duo nobiles in contentionem veniunt, arma capiunt atque uti legem Martis esse arbitrantur, qui victor est primum sanguinem haurit. Itaque quamlibet partem corporis pellere possunt, et ob eam causam vestimenta alba gerunt ut sanguis clare videri possit.

The Epee worship Mars just as the other tribes do. When two noble members enter into a dispute, they take up arms, and as they believe to be the law of Mars, he who draws first blood is the victor. Thus they can hit any part of the body, and for this reason they wear white garments so that blood can be clearly seen.

Modern épée developed from the nineteenth-century épée de combat, used in duels that were often fought until first blood. Its full-body target and lack of right-of-way preserve parts of that dueling tradition.

Ut manus bracchiaque defendant, in pugna longiorem distantiam servant, saepe ita diu procedunt et recedunt sine ictibus aut etiam minis; hic mos a ceteris gentibus irridetur, quarum mos est ferocior esse.

So that they may defend their hands and arms, in battle they keep a longer distance, so often they advance and retreat for a long time without blows or even threats; this custom is mocked by the other tribes, whose custom is to be more aggressive.

Credunt Rudes et Sabrii Martem legem sacram prioritatis edicisse, qua tantum unus singulatim ius aggrediendi datur. Si saepe disputatur quis hoc ius divinum meruerit (et quidem fit), arbitro lis componenda est. Ita hae gentes ferocius pugnant ut utilissimum favorem divinum consequantur. Epera hunc morem haeresim putant. Si et aggressor et defensor simul pellerent, in vero duello ambo perirent, tamen apud Rudes Sabriosque, aggressor vincit.

The Foil and Sabre believe Mars has declared a sacred law of priority, by which only one fencer at a time is granted the right of attacking. If it is disputed who has earned this divine right (and indeed it happens often), the referee must settle the dispute. Thus these tribes fight more aggressively in order to attain the most advantageous divine favor. The Epee think that these customs are heresy. If both attacker and defender were to hit at the same time, in a true duel both would perish, yet among the Foil and Sabre the attacker prevails.

Praeterea, non omnes partes corporis pelli possunt, sed solum eae habitae letales, maxime pectus. Apud Rudes, hoc fit veras pugnas exercendi causa. Sabrii autem se equis imponunt, et turpe equum vulnerare habetur; ita solum supra crura pellunt. Propter velocitatem equorum, Sabrii sunt gens sola quae acie gladii utatur, pro mucrone.

Furthermore, not all parts of the body may be struck, but only those areas considered lethal, especially the chest. Among the Foil, this is done for the sake of practicing for real battles. However, the Sabre mount themselves on horses, and it is considered improper to wound the horse; thus they hit only above the legs. On account of the speed of the horses, the Sabre are the only tribe who use the edge of their sword, instead of the tip.

Sabre fencing developed from cavalry sword traditions. Its cutting actions and upper-body target reflect that mounted-combat history. The idea that wounding the horse is “improper” is the narrator’s comic explanation of the rule rather than a literal historical taboo.

Cum in eodem loco exerceant, hae gentes non pugnant inter se, quoniam eorum leges et mensurae artium non pares sunt. Sunt tamen aliqui qui ad duas aut etiam ad omnes tres gentes pertinent.

Although they train in the same place, these tribes do not fight among themselves, since their laws and measures of skill are not equal. Nevertheless, there are some fencers who are part of two or even all three tribes.
